# Commercial Sales Playbook

## 1. Purpose

This file is the operating reference for commercial work involving:

- positioning;
- market and segment selection;
- acquisition;
- qualification;
- discovery;
- sales conversations;
- demonstrations and trial experiences;
- objections;
- proposals;
- negotiation;
- decision management;
- pipeline;
- account strategy;
- commercial messaging;
- commercial application of ABM.

Use the relevant method rather than applying every framework to every situation.

This playbook synthesizes durable operating principles from:

- *Gap Selling*;
- *Demand-Side Sales 101*;
- *The Challenger Sale*;
- *Account-Based Marketing*;
- *The SaaS Sales Method Fundamentals*.

Consult the original book when the user requests methodological fidelity, when an edge case depends on nuance, or when this playbook does not resolve a conflict.

---

## 2. Method selector

| Decision or problem | Primary method | Supporting method |
|---|---|---|
| Understand why customers start looking | Demand-Side Sales | Customer research |
| Understand the real competitor | Demand-Side Sales | Market analysis |
| Diagnose a live opportunity | Gap Selling | Commercial communication |
| Qualify or disqualify a deal | Gap Selling | Unit economics |
| Build a complex B2B insight | Challenger | Gap Selling |
| Tailor a message to stakeholders | Challenger | Account mapping |
| Conduct outreach or a meeting | SaaS Sales Fundamentals | Relevant primary method |
| Design a B2B target-account program | ABM | RevOps and scale playbook |
| Create a demo or trial experience | Gap Selling | Demand-Side Sales |
| Investigate product or market without selling | Customer research | Use the `market-research` skill (`customer-research-playbook.md`) |
| Build a scaled sales organization | RevOps and scale | Use the `recurring-revenue` skill (`revenue-operations-and-scale-playbook.md`) |

### Selection principle

Choose the method according to the decision and the available evidence.

Do not select a framework because it sounds sophisticated or because it appears in the source library.

# Part I — Commercial foundations

## 3. Selling is a change decision

A sale occurs when a customer decides that moving from the current situation to a different future is worth the cost, effort, risk, and disruption.

Commercial work should therefore clarify:

- what is happening now;
- why the current situation matters;
- what changed or triggered attention;
- what the customer wants to accomplish;
- which alternatives exist;
- what prevents movement;
- what evidence supports change;
- what decision must happen next.

Do not begin with a product pitch when the customer’s change decision is not understood.

---

## 4. Diagnose the system, not only the funnel symptom

A weak commercial result can originate in:

- unclear market definition;
- weak problem intensity;
- wrong segment;
- poor timing;
- insufficient trust;
- unclear positioning;
- weak offer;
- low-quality acquisition;
- poor qualification;
- shallow discovery;
- bad stakeholder coverage;
- ineffective demo;
- unclear buying process;
- implementation anxiety;
- product or service gap;
- delivery capacity;
- bad economics;
- weak retention.

Before prescribing more leads, more calls, lower prices, or a new script, locate the actual constraint.

---

## 5. Evidence hierarchy in commercial work

Use, in descending order:

1. observed customer behavior;
2. direct customer statements about specific events;
3. verified internal data;
4. repeated patterns across interviews and deals;
5. external evidence;
6. informed hypothesis;
7. intuition.

Demographics and firmographics may help locate customers. They do not, by themselves, explain why a customer buys.

---

# Part II — Demand-Side Sales

## 6. Purpose

Use Demand-Side Sales to understand the customer’s side of the market:

- why change begins;
- what causes active search;
- which progress the customer seeks;
- what alternatives are considered;
- which tradeoffs matter;
- what prevents the switch;
- how the customer moves through the decision timeline.

The product is not the unit of analysis. The customer’s progress in context is.

---

## 7. Struggling moment

A struggling moment is the concrete context in which the current way becomes insufficient and the person begins to imagine progress.

Investigate:

- what was happening;
- where the person was;
- who else was involved;
- what changed;
- what made the situation noticeable;
- what workaround existed;
- why the person did not act earlier;
- what event moved the person toward active looking.

Avoid generic labels such as “needs automation” or “wants English.” Reconstruct the specific situation.

---

## 8. Causation rather than correlation

Do not assume that age, role, industry, company size, or another descriptive attribute caused the purchase.

Use descriptive attributes to segment only when they correspond to meaningful differences in:

- struggling moment;
- buying process;
- value;
- urgency;
- service requirements;
- economics.

Seek the sequence of events that caused movement.

---

## 9. Functional, emotional, and social motivations

### Functional

What is the customer trying to get done?

### Emotional

How does the current or desired situation make the customer feel?

### Social

How does the decision affect how the customer is seen by others or by themselves?

A strong diagnosis may include all three dimensions. Do not invent emotional or social motives; obtain evidence.

---

## 10. Forces of Progress

### Push of the current situation

What makes the current way increasingly unacceptable?

### Pull of the new solution

What attractive future or mechanism draws the customer toward change?

### Anxiety of the new solution

What uncertainty, risk, effort, loss, or fear makes the new option difficult?

### Habit of the present

What familiarity, workaround, routine, sunk cost, relationship, or inertia supports the status quo?

### Practical use

A deal may stall because:

- push is weak;
- pull is vague;
- anxiety is high;
- habit is strong.

Identify the dominant force before choosing the intervention.

Do not manufacture fear or exaggerate the customer’s problem.

---

## 11. Buyer timeline

### First Thought

The customer first imagines that life or work could be different.

Commercial implication:

- help the market recognize a relevant struggle;
- use customer language;
- do not demand an immediate purchase.

### Passive Looking

The customer notices the problem and alternatives but has not committed to active search.

Commercial implication:

- help the customer make sense of the struggle;
- surface consequences and tradeoffs;
- remain useful without assuming urgency.

### Active Looking

A triggering event creates deliberate search.

Commercial implication:

- clarify alternatives;
- reduce information overload;
- understand the event and decision criteria;
- help the buyer compare mechanisms, not only features.

### Deciding

The customer resolves tradeoffs and commits.

Commercial implication:

- address anxieties;
- clarify implementation and fit;
- support stakeholder consensus;
- make the next decision explicit.

### Onboarding

The customer begins using the new solution and tests whether it delivers the expected progress.

Commercial implication:

- preserve the context and expectations from the buying process;
- avoid a handoff that forces the customer to repeat the story.

### Ongoing Use

The customer repeatedly decides whether to continue using, paying for, expanding, or replacing the solution.

Commercial implication:

- align Customer Success with the original progress and recurring little hires.

---

## 12. Big hire and little hires

The big hire is the initial decision to adopt or buy.

Little hires are recurring decisions to:

- use;
- attend;
- complete a workflow;
- trust the new process;
- involve another stakeholder;
- continue paying;
- expand.

A sale can close while the customer fails to make the little hires required for value.

Use this concept to connect sales quality with onboarding and retention.

---

## 13. Demand-Side interview outputs

When applying Demand-Side Sales, produce:

- customer situation;
- first thought;
- passive-looking period;
- triggering event;
- active-looking behavior;
- alternatives considered;
- decision tradeoffs;
- functional motivation;
- emotional motivation;
- social motivation;
- push;
- pull;
- anxiety;
- habit;
- big hire;
- early little hires;
- customer language;
- commercial implications.

Use neutral customer research when reconstructing a real buying timeline. Do not turn the interview into a pitch.

# Part III — Gap Selling

## 14. Purpose

Use Gap Selling to diagnose the distance between the customer’s current state and desired future state and determine whether the problem creates enough value to justify change.

The problem drives the sale. Product knowledge does not replace diagnosis.

---

## 15. Current state

Map the current environment in enough detail to understand how the problem is produced.

Possible fields:

- processes;
- people;
- systems;
- inputs;
- outputs;
- volumes;
- frequency;
- timing;
- dependencies;
- exceptions;
- workarounds;
- controls;
- costs;
- risks;
- current metrics.

Do not collect situational facts that do not contribute to diagnosis or qualification.

---

## 16. Problem hierarchy

### Technical or operational problem

The visible difficulty in a process, tool, behavior, or workflow.

### Business problem

The consequence to revenue, cost, time, risk, capacity, customer experience, quality, growth, or strategic performance.

### Individual or stakeholder impact

The consequence to the buyer’s responsibility, credibility, workload, stress, goals, or position.

Do not stop at a feature need or technical symptom.

Example pattern:

- technical problem: documents are manually separated;
- business problem: the cycle consumes capacity and creates error exposure;
- stakeholder impact: the HR leader remains responsible for confidential-data mistakes and delayed work.

Use company-specific evidence rather than assuming the example applies.

---

## 17. Root cause

Ask why the problem exists.

A root cause may involve:

- process design;
- missing information;
- system fragmentation;
- incentives;
- ownership;
- skill;
- capacity;
- policy;
- technology;
- customer behavior;
- market conditions.

Do not prescribe a solution that only treats the symptom.

---

## 18. Impact

Investigate the effect of the problem.

Possible dimensions:

- financial;
- operational;
- customer;
- employee;
- risk;
- strategic;
- personal.

Quantify only when evidence supports the calculation.

A weak or unimportant impact usually produces weak urgency and poor willingness to change.

---

## 19. Future state

Define what the customer wants the environment to become.

Distinguish:

- technical future state;
- business future state;
- stakeholder outcome;
- measurable result;
- required timeline.

The future state should not be defined only as “use our product.”

---

## 20. Intrinsic motivation

Determine what is driving the customer’s change.

Useful prompt:

- What is driving this change now?

Possible motives include:

- business goal;
- risk avoidance;
- professional credibility;
- strategic initiative;
- customer pressure;
- personal workload;
- upcoming event.

Do not infer motivation without evidence.

---

## 21. The gap

The gap is the meaningful distance between current and future states.

Evaluate:

- severity of current problems;
- impact;
- desired outcome;
- urgency;
- cost of inaction;
- cost and risk of change;
- confidence in the solution;
- implementation capacity.

A large feature list does not create a large gap.

---

## 22. Qualification and disqualification

A qualified opportunity normally requires:

- a real problem;
- meaningful impact;
- a desired future state;
- motivation to change;
- sufficient fit;
- ability to implement;
- access to the decision process;
- viable economics.

Disqualify, defer, or reduce effort when:

- there is no meaningful problem;
- the impact is weak;
- the customer is only collecting information;
- the product cannot support the future state;
- expectations are incompatible;
- implementation is impossible;
- decision access is unavailable;
- cost to serve is structurally poor.

Do not artificially enlarge the problem to save an unqualified deal.

---

## 23. Gap Selling discovery flow

Use a conversational sequence rather than a rigid interrogation.

1. Establish relevant current-state context.
2. Identify the visible problem.
3. Explore how the problem affects the business.
4. Determine why the problem exists.
5. Clarify who is affected.
6. Quantify where credible.
7. Define the desired future state.
8. Identify intrinsic motivation and timing.
9. Evaluate the gap and change economics.
10. Clarify decision criteria, stakeholders, process, and next commitment.

Summarize the diagnosis using the customer’s language and ask for confirmation.

---

## 24. Demonstration design

A demo should show how the solution addresses the diagnosed gap.

Structure:

1. restate the current state and desired outcome;
2. confirm what matters most;
3. show only the relevant workflow;
4. connect each capability to the problem and impact;
5. address implementation anxieties;
6. identify unsupported requirements honestly;
7. agree on evidence and next decision.

Do not conduct a generic product tour.

---

## 25. Proposal design

A proposal should contain:

- customer context;
- current-state diagnosis;
- business problem and impact;
- desired future state;
- recommended scope;
- mechanism of value;
- responsibilities;
- implementation assumptions;
- success criteria;
- commercial terms;
- risks or exclusions;
- next decision.

Do not use a proposal to compensate for weak discovery.

---

## 26. Price objections

A price objection may indicate:

- weak problem impact;
- unclear value;
- poor fit;
- high anxiety;
- low urgency;
- missing decision authority;
- package mismatch;
- real budget constraint;
- competitive alternative;
- weak differentiation.

Process:

1. clarify what “too expensive” means;
2. reconnect to the problem and impact;
3. compare cost, risk, and value;
4. assess whether scope or package should change;
5. do not defend the price reflexively;
6. disqualify when the gap does not justify the investment.

---

## 27. Deal review

For each opportunity, determine:

- current state known;
- problem known;
- impact known;
- root cause known;
- future state known;
- motivation known;
- gap meaningful;
- stakeholders identified;
- decision process understood;
- evidence required;
- next customer commitment;
- risk;
- disqualification reason if applicable.

Do not forecast from seller optimism alone.

---

# Part IV — Challenger

## 28. Purpose and activation conditions

Use Challenger when a complex B2B buyer would benefit from a credible insight that changes how they understand a material business problem.

Challenger is most appropriate when:

- the sale is complex;
- several stakeholders are involved;
- the status quo is strong;
- risk aversion is high;
- the customer needs help navigating change;
- the company has insight based on evidence;
- the insight can lead to a differentiated capability.

Do not use Challenger by default in B2C, low-value, straightforward, or simple service purchases.

---

## 29. Teach, tailor, and take control

### Teach

Offer an insight that helps the customer understand their business differently.

A useful commercial insight should:

- concern a material customer problem;
- challenge an assumption;
- be supported by evidence;
- create a better decision criterion;
- imply action;
- connect to a credible mechanism of value.

### Tailor

Adapt the insight to:

- role;
- responsibilities;
- metrics;
- business priorities;
- risks;
- personal and organizational outcomes.

Do not send the same message to every stakeholder.

### Take control

Guide the decision by:

- clarifying the buying process;
- identifying required stakeholders;
- defining evaluation criteria;
- surfacing risk;
- resisting unsupported concessions;
- establishing a concrete next action.

Taking control is not aggression. It is decision guidance.

---

## 30. Commercial Teaching conditions

A Commercial Teaching insight should:

1. lead to a material business implication;
2. challenge the customer’s current thinking;
3. support action rather than only interest;
4. be repeatable across a relevant segment;
5. connect to a differentiated capability or approach.

Do not create a dramatic reframe with unsupported data.

---

## 31. Purposeful choreography

A complex teaching conversation may follow:

1. **Warmer** — begin with familiar customer challenges and a credible hypothesis.
2. **Reframe** — introduce a different way to understand the problem.
3. **Rational evidence** — establish the business consequence with defensible logic or data.
4. **Emotional impact** — make the consequence concrete for the relevant stakeholders.
5. **New way** — describe the capabilities or behaviors required to solve the problem.
6. **Your solution** — show how the offer supports the new way.

Lead to the solution rather than leading with the solution.

Use the sequence selectively. A normal conversation does not require a theatrical presentation.

---

## 32. Challenger guardrails

- Diagnose before challenging when the insight is not validated.
- Do not manufacture fear.
- Do not humiliate the buyer.
- Do not confuse assertiveness with pressure.
- Do not overstate customer data.
- Do not use a reframe that cannot lead to value the company can deliver.
- Do not treat relationship quality as irrelevant; value creation should earn trust.

---

# Part V — Customer-centric communication

## 33. Core interaction behavior

Use the following behaviors across email, calls, meetings, demos, and Customer Success conversations:

- prepare;
- establish relevance;
- use an appropriate tone;
- ask thoughtful questions;
- listen;
- take concise notes;
- use the customer’s language;
- elaborate on important statements;
- mirror and summarize;
- define the next step.

Do not use the time while the customer speaks to prepare the next pitch.

---

## 34. Question design

### Situational questions

Use to establish necessary facts or confirm research.

Avoid asking many questions that could have been answered before the meeting.

### Problem questions

Use to understand difficulty, friction, failure, or dissatisfaction.

Make them specific to the customer’s context.

### Impact questions

Use after a relevant problem is understood.

Examples of forms:

- What effect does that have on…?
- How often does that cause…?
- What happens when…?
- What is the result for…?

Do not ask impact questions before enough trust and context exist.

### Outcome questions

Clarify what success would change.

### Decision questions

Clarify stakeholders, criteria, process, timing, and next action.

---

## 35. Listening and summarizing

Use customer language accurately.

A useful summary:

- reflects the situation;
- names the problem;
- captures the impact;
- avoids premature solution language;
- asks whether the understanding is correct.

Do not respond enthusiastically to negative customer experiences in a way that sounds insensitive.

---

## 36. RRR written communication

Use RRR as a compact structure for relevant outreach.

### Relevance

Show why the message is relevant to the person or account.

### Reward

Offer useful insight, evidence, content, or a plausible benefit from engaging.

### Request

Make a clear and proportionate next-step request.

Avoid confusing token personalization with relevance.

Avoid:

- deceptive subject lines;
- generic claims;
- long company introductions;
- excessive “I,” “we,” and “our” language;
- a request disproportionate to the value offered.

---

## 37. ACE meeting opening

Use ACE when useful:

### Appreciate

Acknowledge the participant’s time.

### Check End Time

Confirm the available time.

### End Goal

State or confirm the outcome the meeting should produce.

At the end:

- assess whether the goal was met;
- define the next decision;
- identify required participants;
- connect the next meeting to a clear purpose.

---

## 38. Meeting preparation

Before a material meeting:

- research the company, role, market, and relevant event;
- define the meeting goal;
- identify the method being used;
- prepare hypotheses, not assumptions;
- decide what evidence is required;
- identify likely stakeholders and anxieties;
- prepare only the relevant materials;
- reserve time for immediate follow-up.

---

## 39. Follow-up

A useful follow-up should be easy for the champion to share internally.

Include:

- key observations;
- confirmed diagnosis or decisions;
- open questions;
- action items;
- owner;
- timing;
- next meeting goal.

Keep it concise and accurate.

---

# Part VI — Commercial ABM

## 40. Purpose

ABM is a B2B strategy for concentrating coordinated commercial and marketing effort on selected accounts that matter economically.

It is not merely:

- adding a company name to an email;
- running personalized advertising;
- creating a list of famous companies;
- assigning every enterprise lead to a salesperson.

---

## 41. ABM readiness

Before creating an ABM program, confirm:

- B2B context;
- identifiable accounts;
- account value that justifies concentrated effort;
- a target-market or ICP hypothesis;
- capacity for account research;
- Sales and Marketing coordination;
- data and ownership;
- a measurable pilot period.

Do not apply ABM to a high-volume individual-consumer market.

---

## 42. Six ABM operating areas

### Identify

Build and govern the target-account list.

### Attract

Create relevant awareness among selected accounts.

### Engage

Deliver coordinated, role-relevant experiences and messages.

### Convert

Identify meaningful buying signals and create qualified commercial engagement.

### Close

Support stakeholder consensus and decision progression.

### Measure

Evaluate account engagement, pipeline, revenue, and learning.

---

## 43. Target-account list

A target-account list should include:

- account name;
- inclusion rationale;
- segment or tier;
- expected value;
- problem hypothesis;
- known stakeholders;
- owner;
- current relationship;
- intent or triggering signals when available;
- next action;
- review date.

### Selection criteria

Possible criteria:

- fit;
- problem intensity;
- strategic initiative;
- revenue potential;
- implementation fit;
- retention potential;
- expansion potential;
- relationship;
- timing;
- data quality.

Avoid selecting accounts only by size or brand recognition.

---

## 44. Account tiers

Use tiers to match effort with value and complexity.

### One-to-one

High-value accounts with significant account-specific research and orchestration.

### One-to-few

Groups of accounts sharing a meaningful pattern, industry, use case, or trigger.

### One-to-many

Scaled account-based experiences for a larger defined set.

Do not create tiers without an explicit difference in treatment and economics.

---

## 45. ABM pilot

Start with a bounded pilot.

Define:

- objective;
- target accounts;
- period;
- owners;
- account hypotheses;
- channels;
- stakeholder coverage;
- success measures;
- review cadence;
- exit or scale criteria.

The pilot should test whether focused account work creates better commercial outcomes than the relevant alternative.

---

## 46. Account plan

For a priority account, include:

- company context;
- strategic initiatives;
- account problem hypothesis;
- current alternatives;
- stakeholders and roles;
- relationship strength;
- Commercial Teaching hypothesis if justified;
- value hypothesis;
- engagement plan;
- risks;
- next commitment;
- evidence and learning.

Do not present unverified account research as fact.

---

## 47. ABM metrics

Possible metrics include:

- target accounts engaged;
- stakeholder coverage;
- meaningful account activity;
- meetings;
- opportunities;
- pipeline represented by target accounts;
- stage progression;
- sales cycle;
- win rate;
- average deal size;
- revenue;
- expansion;
- pilot learning.

Avoid evaluating ABM only by lead volume.

Use the `recurring-revenue` skill (`revenue-operations-and-scale-playbook.md`) for organizational implementation, technology, dashboards, and scale.

# Part VII — Integrated commercial workflow

## 48. From market learning to revenue

A useful integrated sequence is:

1. **Research** — understand customer behavior without selling.
2. **Demand-Side synthesis** — identify struggling moments, forces, timeline, and alternatives.
3. **Segment and position** — define who, when, and why.
4. **Acquisition** — reach customers in relevant contexts.
5. **Qualification** — confirm fit and potential value.
6. **Gap diagnosis** — establish current state, impact, future state, and motivation.
7. **Challenger insight** — use only when a credible reframe improves a complex B2B decision.
8. **Demo or trial** — prove relevance to the diagnosed problem.
9. **Decision management** — clarify stakeholders, evidence, process, and next action.
10. **Handoff** — transfer the purchase context to Customer Success.

Do not collapse research, discovery, and teaching into one undifferentiated conversation.

### Consult *Gap Selling* when:

- designing a full discovery methodology;
- coaching a sales team;
- building a deal-review or pipeline-inspection system;
- resolving questions about current state, future state, impact, and intrinsic motivation.

### Consult *Demand-Side Sales 101* when:

- reconstructing a switching timeline;
- designing progress-based positioning;
- connecting sales, onboarding, and recurring use;
- analyzing struggling moments or real competition.

### Consult *The Challenger Sale* when:

- building a Commercial Teaching insight;
- designing a complex stakeholder narrative;
- coaching teach, tailor, or take-control behavior;
- evaluating whether a reframe is defensible.

### Consult *The SaaS Sales Method Fundamentals* when:

- designing detailed interaction scripts;
- creating a meeting system;

## 51. Quality check

Before finalizing commercial advice, verify that:

- the customer situation is grounded;
- the real decision and stage are understood;
- research was not confused with selling;
- the problem and impact were diagnosed before prescribing;
- the selected method fits the context;
- Challenger is supported by a credible insight;
- ABM economics justify account-level effort;
- price is not being treated as the cause without diagnosis;
- the next customer commitment is explicit;
- the recommendation does not exceed delivery capacity;
- facts, hypotheses, and unknowns remain separate;
- current tactics or benchmarks are verified when needed.
