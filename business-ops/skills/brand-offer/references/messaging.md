# Messaging and page copy

How to shape copy written for an audience: pages, campaign e-mails, ads and posts. `brand-offer`, `marketing`, `ads`, `social-media` and `material-review` use it. A one-to-one message (a reply to a lead, a follow-up) follows the `sales` skill instead, because it answers a person and not an audience.

Everything here sits on "Evidence before framework" and the claim and promise test, in section 3 of `../business-context/references/advisor-protocol.md` (relative to the skill folder). The structures below decide what to say first and what to leave out; they never supply a pain, a result or an urgency the evidence does not.

## Awareness stage

Schwartz's levels of awareness describe how much the reader already knows about the problem and its solutions. Use the stage to decide:

- how much context to explain;
- whether to open with the problem, the mechanism, the category or the product;
- which objections probably need an answer;
- which call to action is proportionate.

| The reader | Open with | Usually needs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Does not yet see the problem | A situation or consequence they recognize from their own week | Why it matters; the product can wait |
| Sees the problem, not the kind of solution | The problem in their words, then the mechanism that solves it | How it works and why it is believable; the product name comes later |
| Knows the kind of solution, not this one | What is different here, and for whom | Comparison, proof, risk |
| Knows this product | The offer: plan, condition, next step | Price, terms, the last anxiety removed |
| Ready to act | The action | The shortest path to it |

Limits of the tool:

- **The stage comes from evidence:** where the reader arrives from (a search term, an ad, a referral, an existing customer), what they asked, how the company's customers describe their first contact. When it is unclear, say so and write for the less aware reading, or ask.
- **It describes what the reader knows, not who they are.** It is not a psychological segment, and it changes as the person learns.
- **It says nothing about pain, desire or urgency.** Knowing the problem does not mean suffering from it now; how much it hurts comes from evidence.
- **It guides the message.** It does not replace research (`market-research`) or the diagnosis of a live deal (`sales`).

## Framework-shaped copy

Text written to fill a framework rather than to speak to a person. A reader senses it even without naming it, and trust drops. Signs:

- a problem dramatized beyond what customers actually described;
- a grand transformation where a practical improvement is what is on offer;
- benefits stacked one per slot, with no order of importance;
- a call to action in every message or every paragraph;
- urgency with no real event behind it (a deadline, a price change, limited capacity the company confirms);
- the problem and solution contrast repeated throughout the piece;
- emotional language in an operational subject (payroll, compliance, logistics) where the reader wants facts;
- "Imagine if..." with no scene from the reader's real situation;
- rhetorical questions the reader would not ask;
- text that could be sent to anyone by changing the name.

The sentence-level tells (the staged "it is not X, it is Y", "The result?", lists of three by reflex, words like unlock, revolutionize, empower) are listed under "Patterns that sound generated" in `../social-media/references/formats.md` and apply to every channel.

The fix is the same for all of them: go back to the evidence and say the specific thing, at the size it really has.

## Pages and campaign e-mails

A page is built from a menu of components, not a fixed sequence:

headline, subheadline, problem or context, mechanism (how it works), differentiation, desired outcome, proof, cases or evidence, objections, risk and how it is reduced, offer, scope (what is and is not included), call to action, FAQ.

A good page may use a few of them. Choose which and in what order from:

- the awareness stage of the people the page receives;
- how complex and how expensive the purchase is;
- the risk the buyer perceives;
- the proof that exists;
- how familiar the category is;
- the stage of the decision;
- the acquisition channel: the page continues the ad, search or e-mail that brought the reader, so the headline answers what made them click.

Rules:

- Each section answers a question this reader has at that point. A section with nothing true to say is left out, not filled.
- No proof section without proof: no placeholder testimonial, no "trusted by" without customers authorized to be named, no number the company cannot show.
- A cheap, familiar purchase gets a short page with the offer near the top. An expensive or risky one needs more mechanism, proof, scope and risk reduction before the ask.
- Write in customer language when it exists (interviews, reviews, sales conversations, support tickets). When it does not, describe plainly what the product does, mark the problem framing as a hypothesis, and suggest how to test it.
- Run the claim and promise test on the finished page, including the problem it describes.

What changes by page type:

| Page | Job |
| --- | --- |
| Homepage | Several audiences and stages: what the company does, for whom, and where each visitor goes next |
| Landing page | One audience, one offer, one action, continuing the source that brought the visitor |
| Product page | What it does, for whom, with scope and limits |
| Offer or sales page | The offer and its terms, objections and risk, for readers who already know the product |
| Campaign e-mail | One idea and one action; the subject line tells the truth about the content |
