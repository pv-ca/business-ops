# Customer Success Lifecycle Playbook

## 1. Purpose

This file is the operating reference for:

- pre-sale outcome alignment;
- sales-to-CS handoff;
- customer segmentation;
- onboarding;
- implementation;
- activation;
- time to value;
- adoption;
- customer education;
- engagement;
- value realization and recognition;
- health and risk;
- retention;
- renewal;
- expansion;
- advocacy and referrals;
- churn analysis;
- Voice of Customer;
- Customer Success Operations.

This playbook integrates durable principles from:

- *Demand-Side Sales 101*;
- *Gap Selling*;
- *Blueprints for a SaaS Sales Organization*;
- *Deploy Empathy*;
- the broader Customer Success discipline.

Use it as an operating system, not as a requirement to create every process, meeting, score, or role described here.

---

## 2. Definition

Customer Success is the disciplined function of helping suitable customers achieve the outcomes that justify continuing and, when appropriate, expanding the commercial relationship.

Customer Success is not equivalent to:

- reactive support;
- generic relationship management;
- frequent check-ins without purpose;
- customer appeasement;
- unlimited service;
- indiscriminate discounting;
- preventing every cancellation;
- disguised selling without customer value.

### Functional distinctions

- **Sales** creates and converts qualified demand.
- **Customer Success** helps customers realize and recognize the value for which they purchased.
- **Customer Support** resolves incidents, questions, and operational problems.
- **Customer Experience** concerns the customer’s overall perception across touchpoints.
- **Product** provides the capabilities and experience through which value is delivered.
- **Operations** creates the systems and capacity required for reliable delivery.

Do not assign every post-sale problem to Customer Success.

---

## 3. Lifecycle principle

The customer relationship should be analyzed as one connected journey.

A retention problem may originate in:

- targeting;
- qualification;
- expectation setting;
- product fit;
- commercial promises;
- implementation;
- onboarding;
- service quality;
- support;
- product design;
- pricing;
- customer capacity;
- customer circumstances.

The close is not the finish line in a recurring-revenue business. It is the transition into value delivery.

---

## 4. Big hire and little hires

### Big hire

The customer makes the initial commitment to a product or service as a way to make progress.

### Little hires

After purchase, the customer repeatedly decides to:

- complete implementation;
- use the product or attend the service;
- follow the recommended workflow;
- involve another stakeholder;
- trust the process;
- renew;
- expand;
- refer others.

A customer can complete the big hire while failing to make the little hires required for value.

Customer Success should identify which recurring decisions predict progress and continuation.

---

## 5. Customer outcome model

For each segment or use case, define:

- customer context;
- desired progress;
- current state;
- business or personal problem;
- impact;
- future state;
- success criteria;
- customer responsibilities;
- company responsibilities;
- dependencies;
- time horizon;
- evidence of progress;
- risk factors.

Do not define customer success only as product usage or contract renewal.

---

# Part I — Before onboarding

## 6. Pre-sale alignment

Before the sale is completed, clarify:

- why the customer is buying;
- the use case;
- desired outcome;
- current-state problems;
- expected timeline;
- stakeholders;
- decision criteria;
- scope;
- customer responsibilities;
- company responsibilities;
- dependencies;
- risks;
- success measures;
- promises and exclusions.

### Expectation debt

Expectation debt is created when sales commitments, assumptions, or implied outcomes exceed what delivery can support.

Prevent it by:

- documenting commitments;
- confirming scope;
- involving delivery when complexity requires it;
- refusing unsupported promises;
- identifying customer dependencies;
- confirming what success does and does not mean.

---

## 7. Customer fit

Customer Success cannot reliably compensate for poor-fit acquisition.

Evaluate:

- problem intensity;
- offer fit;
- required capabilities;
- customer readiness;
- implementation capacity;
- stakeholder availability;
- economics;
- expected cost to serve;
- timeline;
- expectation compatibility.

A bad-fit customer may produce revenue but also:

- implementation delay;
- support burden;
- low adoption;
- dissatisfaction;
- churn;
- reputational risk;
- roadmap distortion.

---

## 8. Sales-to-CS handoff

A useful handoff should transfer:

- customer context;
- desired outcome;
- current state;
- problem and impact;
- use case;
- stakeholders and roles;
- decision criteria;
- objections and anxieties;
- promises;
- exclusions;
- commercial terms relevant to delivery;
- implementation dependencies;
- expected timeline;
- success criteria;
- known risks;
- expansion hypotheses.

The receiving team should know:

- why the customer bought;
- what progress means;
- what was promised;
- what must happen first;
- what could cause failure.

Do not make the customer repeat the entire buying discovery.

### Handoff quality check

A handoff is incomplete when it contains only:

- contract value;
- plan purchased;
- contact details;
- generic implementation notes.

---

# Part II — Segmentation and service model

## 9. Customer segmentation

Segment customers when differences materially affect:

- desired outcomes;
- complexity;
- implementation;
- risk;
- service level;
- cost to serve;
- revenue;
- expansion potential;
- required expertise;
- communication cadence.

Possible dimensions:

- use case;
- revenue;
- complexity;
- strategic value;
- maturity;
- stakeholder count;
- implementation need;
- risk;
- growth potential;
- customer behavior.

Do not segment only by contract value when complexity or risk differs materially.

---

## 10. Service models

Possible models include:

- high touch;
- mid touch;
- pooled;
- tech touch;
- community-led;
- hybrid.

Choose according to:

- customer need;
- economics;
- complexity;
- scale;
- risk;
- available capacity.

Do not provide the same service to every customer by default.

Do not automate a high-risk relationship simply because automation is available.

---

# Part III — Onboarding and implementation

## 11. Onboarding purpose

Onboarding should connect the purchase decision to the first meaningful customer outcome.

It should:

- align expectations;
- establish responsibilities;
- complete necessary setup;
- reduce early friction;
- create momentum;
- reach first value;
- prepare for continued adoption.

Do not treat onboarding as a checklist of internal tasks disconnected from customer progress.

---

## 12. Distinguish lifecycle milestones

These are not the same event:

- contract signed;
- payment completed;
- account created;
- implementation started;
- data prepared;
- configuration completed;
- users invited;
- training delivered;
- activation achieved;
- first value achieved;
- habitual adoption established.

Measure each milestone only when it informs ownership or action.

---

## 13. Onboarding design

Define:

- customer outcome;
- starting point;
- required milestones;
- customer owner;
- company owner;
- customer responsibilities;
- company responsibilities;
- dependencies;
- target timeline;
- risk triggers;
- escalation path;
- activation event;
- first-value event;
- completion criteria.

### Simplification principle

Remove setup that does not materially contribute to value, risk control, or future scale.

Do not automate an unclear or defective onboarding process.

---

## 14. Implementation risk

Potential risks include:

- missing data;
- unavailable stakeholder;
- unclear ownership;
- technical dependency;
- integration difficulty;
- configuration complexity;
- low customer capacity;
- expectation mismatch;
- internal change resistance;
- training overload;
- weak use case.

For each risk define:

- signal;
- owner;
- intervention;
- escalation condition;
- effect on timeline or outcome.

---

## 15. Activation

Activation is the behavior or milestone indicating that the customer has begun experiencing meaningful value and is more likely to continue.

A strong activation event is:

- observable;
- connected to the purchased outcome;
- achievable early;
- specific;
- plausibly predictive of continued engagement or retention.

Activation is not automatically:

- payment;
- account creation;
- login;
- attendance at training;
- completion of every setup task.

### Activation validation

Compare candidate activation behavior with:

- continued use;
- retention;
- renewal;
- progress;
- satisfaction;
- support demand.

Revise the definition when it does not predict value.

---

## 16. Time to value

Time to value is the period from the relevant start point to the customer’s first meaningful outcome.

Choose the start point explicitly, such as:

- commercial commitment;
- onboarding start;
- receipt of required customer data;
- account activation.

Measure from the customer’s perspective.

Possible delays include:

- unnecessary setup;
- unclear ownership;
- unavailable data;
- technical dependency;
- stakeholder absence;
- low customer capacity;
- training friction;
- weak use case;
- expectation mismatch.

Reduce the largest necessary delay rather than hiding incomplete work.

---

# Part IV — Adoption and value

## 17. Adoption

Adoption is meaningful customer behavior connected to value.

Possible dimensions:

- breadth of use;
- depth of use;
- frequency;
- consistency;
- workflow completion;
- stakeholder participation;
- quality of execution;
- outcome achievement.

Do not use login frequency alone as proof of adoption.

### Adoption diagnosis

Low adoption may result from:

- weak fit;
- unclear value;
- poor onboarding;
- absent stakeholder;
- difficult workflow;
- missing capability;
- competing priority;
- insufficient training;
- weak habit;
- low urgency;
- customer capacity;
- service inconsistency.

Do not solve every adoption problem with more training.

---

## 18. Customer education

Customer education should enable behavior that creates value.

Possible formats:

- onboarding guidance;
- documentation;
- contextual instructions;
- short videos;
- live training;
- office hours;
- examples;
- templates;
- community;
- certification when justified.

For each education asset define:

- audience;
- behavior enabled;
- lifecycle stage;
- expected outcome;
- maintenance owner.

Do not produce content without a defined customer behavior or decision.

---

## 19. Value realization

Value realization means the customer has actually received a meaningful result.

Possible evidence:

- time reduced;
- risk reduced;
- workflow completed;
- revenue created or protected;
- cost reduced;
- capacity increased;
- quality improved;
- confidence increased;
- learning progress achieved;
- operational visibility improved.

Use credible baselines and customer-specific evidence.

---

## 20. Value recognition

A customer may realize value without the relevant decision-maker recognizing it.

Value recognition requires:

- credible evidence;
- connection to the original objective;
- communication in stakeholder-relevant language;
- visibility at the appropriate time.

Distinguish:

- value delivered;
- value realized;
- value measured;
- value communicated;
- value recognized.

Do not inflate ROI claims or attribute outcomes the company did not cause.

---

## 21. Success planning

For sufficiently complex customers, a success plan may include:

- desired outcome;
- baseline;
- target;
- milestones;
- measures;
- responsibilities;
- dependencies;
- risks;
- review cadence.

For low-complexity customers, a lightweight objective and next milestone may be sufficient.

Avoid ceremonial success plans that are not used to guide decisions.

---

# Part V — Engagement, health, and risk

## 22. Engagement cadence

Every interaction should have a defined purpose.

Possible purposes:

- remove a blocker;
- review progress;
- align stakeholders;
- manage risk;
- plan a milestone;
- share a relevant insight;
- prepare renewal;
- identify an appropriate expansion.

Choose cadence according to:

- lifecycle stage;
- complexity;
- risk;
- value;
- customer need;
- economics.

Do not schedule meetings merely to appear proactive.

---

## 23. Customer health

Customer health is an interpretable view of whether the customer is likely to make progress and continue.

Potential dimensions:

- activation;
- meaningful behavior;
- outcome achievement;
- stakeholder engagement;
- support patterns;
- payment behavior;
- sentiment;
- implementation status;
- unresolved risks;
- organizational change;
- renewal conditions.

### Health-score design

Start with a few simple signals.

For each signal define:

- why it matters;
- data source;
- threshold;
- owner;
- response playbook;
- evidence that it predicts risk or progress.

Do not create false precision through a complex score without predictive evidence.

---

## 24. Risk response

When a risk signal appears:

1. verify that the signal is real;
2. identify the root cause;
3. determine customer and business impact;
4. assign ownership;
5. choose the smallest effective intervention;
6. define follow-up timing or condition;
7. record the learning.

Do not send generic “checking in” messages when a specific risk is known.

---

## 25. Support patterns

Support data may reveal:

- product defects;
- unclear documentation;
- onboarding gaps;
- customer misunderstanding;
- missing capability;
- recurring workflow friction;
- low customer maturity.

High support volume can mean strong engagement or serious friction. Interpret context before treating it as positive or negative.

---

# Part VI — Retention, renewal, and expansion

## 26. Retention

Retention is the consequence of:

- customer fit;
- continued need;
- expectation alignment;
- value realization;
- value recognition;
- acceptable effort;
- suitable price;
- reliable delivery;
- stakeholder support;
- competitive alternatives;
- customer circumstances.

Do not treat retention as a last-minute save function.

---

## 27. Renewal

Prepare for renewal before the decision becomes urgent.

Evaluate:

- original desired outcome;
- progress achieved;
- value recognized;
- adoption;
- stakeholder support;
- open risks;
- product or service gaps;
- budget;
- decision process;
- timing;
- alternatives;
- commercial terms.

A renewal should be the consequence of demonstrated value and managed expectations, not only a negotiation event.

### Renewal plan

Include:

- renewal date or decision window;
- decision-maker;
- champion;
- required evidence;
- value summary;
- open risks;
- commercial conditions;
- next actions;
- owner.

---

## 28. Expansion

Expansion may include:

- more users;
- more units;
- increased usage;
- higher frequency;
- higher plan;
- additional workflow;
- additional product;
- additional stakeholder group;
- cross-sell;
- referral or partnership.

Recommend expansion when:

- the initial use case has demonstrated value;
- another relevant need exists;
- the customer has capacity to adopt;
- the additional offer supports customer progress;
- economics are healthy;
- risk is acceptable.

Expansion should deepen customer value before increasing revenue.

Do not pressure unhealthy customers into expansion.

### Expansion signals

Potential signals include:

- successful use case;
- capacity limit;
- new team or location;
- repeated relevant request;
- increased engagement;
- new objective;
- stakeholder interest;
- organizational growth.

---

## 29. Advocacy and referrals

Advocacy may include:

- testimonial;
- review;
- case study;
- reference call;
- community participation;
- referral.

Ask when:

- credible value exists;
- the customer is satisfied;
- timing is appropriate;
- effort is proportionate;
- permission and privacy are clear.

Do not manufacture advocacy before value.

Separate research interviews from testimonial requests.

---

# Part VII — Churn

## 30. Churn classification

Classify churn before responding.

Possible types:

- preventable;
- structural;
- bad-fit;
- involuntary;
- price-related;
- value-perception;
- adoption failure;
- implementation failure;
- product or service gap;
- stakeholder change;
- financial distress;
- competitive replacement;
- temporary pause;
- seasonal or use-case completion.

Do not classify every cancellation as a Customer Success failure.

---

## 31. Churn diagnosis

For each case, identify:

- stated reason;
- observed evidence;
- deeper cause;
- original use case;
- acquisition source;
- fit;
- promises;
- onboarding history;
- activation status;
- adoption pattern;
- support history;
- value realized;
- value recognized;
- stakeholder changes;
- payment or budget conditions;
- alternative chosen;
- first warning signal;
- preventability.

Use neutral cancellation research when the cause is unclear.

---

## 32. Churn reduction

Prioritize systemic causes using:

- frequency;
- revenue impact;
- preventability;
- customer impact;
- cost of correction;
- strategic importance.

Possible interventions may affect:

- targeting;
- qualification;
- promises;
- onboarding;
- product;
- service delivery;
- education;
- communication;
- pricing;
- risk detection;
- capacity.

Do not use discounts to hide structural failure.

---

## 33. Save conversations

A save conversation is not a research interview.

When attempting retention:

1. clarify the reason;
2. assess whether the customer is a suitable fit;
3. identify whether the problem is correctable;
4. avoid promising what cannot be delivered;
5. offer a proportionate solution;
6. accept appropriate churn when continuation would be harmful.

Do not retain every customer at any cost.

---

# Part VIII — Voice of Customer and operations

## 34. Voice of Customer

Possible sources:

- customer interviews;
- support;
- sales calls;
- onboarding;
- surveys;
- cancellation research;
- reviews;
- usage data;
- customer meetings;
- teacher or CSM observations;
- communities.

Analyze feedback by:

- segment;
- role;
- use case;
- lifecycle stage;
- frequency;
- impact;
- strategic fit;
- willingness to pay;
- evidence quality.

Distinguish:

- problem;
- requested solution;
- workaround;
- desired outcome;
- emotional reaction.

Do not convert every request into a roadmap commitment.

---

## 35. Closing the feedback loop

When appropriate:

- acknowledge the input;
- explain what was learned;
- communicate the decision without overpromising;
- update the customer when action occurs;
- preserve a record for future analysis.

Do not promise a delivery date without authority and evidence.

---

## 36. Customer Success Operations

CS Operations may own or support:

- lifecycle definitions;
- playbooks;
- segmentation;
- capacity planning;
- systems;
- data quality;
- health signals;
- automation;
- reporting;
- renewal workflows;
- forecasting;
- knowledge management;
- governance.

### Automation principles

Automate:

- reminders;
- routine education;
- data movement;
- routing;
- low-risk standard communication;
- alerts.

Preserve human judgment for:

- complex risk;
- trust;
- sensitive communication;
- negotiation;
- ambiguity;
- strategic accounts;
- educational or personal motivation issues.

---

## 37. Capacity planning

Customer Success capacity should reflect:

- customer volume;
- service model;
- complexity;
- onboarding effort;
- support burden;
- risk;
- communication cadence;
- expansion responsibility;
- automation;
- expected quality.

Do not assign account counts using a generic benchmark without understanding work demand.

# Part IX — Metrics

## 38. Metric design

A metric is useful when it informs a decision.

For each metric define:

- question answered;
- formula;
- data source;
- owner;
- frequency;
- threshold;
- action.

Do not create dashboards containing metrics without an owner or response.

---

## 39. Potential recurring-revenue metrics

Depending on business model:

- onboarding completion;
- activation rate;
- implementation duration;
- time to value;
- adoption;
- health distribution;
- logo retention;
- customer churn;
- revenue churn;
- gross revenue retention;
- net revenue retention;
- renewal rate;
- expansion;
- contraction;
- support burden;
- cost to serve;
- customer satisfaction;
- referral rate.

Definitions must remain consistent across periods.

---

## 40. Basic retention formulas

### Customer churn

Lost customers during the period divided by customers at the start of the period, using a consistent eligibility definition.

### Logo retention

Retained eligible customers divided by customers eligible to renew.

### Gross Revenue Retention

Starting recurring revenue minus churn and contraction, divided by starting recurring revenue.

### Net Revenue Retention

Starting recurring revenue minus churn and contraction plus expansion, divided by starting recurring revenue.

Do not compare these metrics across companies without consistent definitions.

---

## 41. B2C service and education metrics

Possible metrics include:

- active students or customers;
- first-month retention;
- monthly retention;
- attendance;
- repeated absence;
- trial-to-enrollment conversion;
- average frequency;
- cancellation;
- reactivation;
- referral;
- teacher or service-provider utilization;
- available capacity;
- delinquency;
- reported progress.

Avoid applying enterprise SaaS metrics mechanically to individual educational relationships.

# Part X — Company-specific adaptation

## 42. Reading the company's own adaptation

The user's own companies differ in model, buyer and stage, so this playbook stays general. When the company's folder in the context folder holds `method-adaptation.md`, read the part about this subject before recommending: it records how the method applies to that company (who to interview, what constrains growth, which practices are too heavy for it). When there is no such file and the adaptation matters, work it out from the company's context file and offer to record it there.

## 45. When to consult original sources

Consult *Demand-Side Sales 101* when:

- analyzing ongoing little hires;
- connecting purchase causes to onboarding and use;
- understanding replacement or switching behavior.

Consult *Gap Selling* when:

- preserving current state, future state, impact, and motivation across handoff;
- analyzing whether the original problem was sufficiently diagnosed.

Consult *Blueprints for a SaaS Sales Organization* when:

- connecting Customer Success to Moments That Matter;
- designing scaled lifecycle roles, costs, capacity, and recurring-revenue architecture.

Consult *Deploy Empathy* when:

- designing new-customer, long-term customer, or cancellation interviews;
- separating neutral research from save, support, or success conversations.

---

## 46. Quality check

Before finalizing Customer Success advice, verify that:

- the customer is suitable for the offer;
- the original desired outcome is known;
- sales promises are documented;
- onboarding is connected to value;
- activation is behavioral and testable;
- usage is not confused with outcome;
- value realization and recognition are distinguished;
- health signals are interpretable;
- churn is classified before intervention;
- renewal starts before the deadline;
- expansion follows initial value;
- research is separated from save conversations;
- service design fits the business model and economics;
- metrics have owners and actions;
- facts, hypotheses, and unknowns remain separate.
