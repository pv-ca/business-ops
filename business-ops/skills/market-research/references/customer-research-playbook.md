# Customer Research Playbook

## 1. Purpose

This file is the operating reference for qualitative customer research involving:

- problem discovery;
- idea validation;
- product discovery;
- customer-process understanding;
- buying and switching research;
- new-customer interviews;
- long-term customer interviews;
- cancellation interviews;
- interactive or usability-oriented interviews;
- prioritization research;
- willingness-to-pay research;
- feature-request analysis;
- continuous Voice of Customer.

The principal methodology source is *Deploy Empathy* by Michele Hansen. Demand-Side Sales may support switching research, but neutral research behavior remains governed by this playbook.

Consult the original book when a detailed script, difficult interview situation, recruitment problem, or specialized research design requires more nuance.

---

## 2. Research is not sales, support, or onboarding

### Customer research

Seeks reliable learning about a person’s real process, context, behavior, problems, tradeoffs, and decision.

### Sales discovery

Diagnoses a live commercial opportunity and may lead to a recommendation or purchase decision.

### Customer Support

Resolves a current problem.

### Onboarding

Helps a customer begin using a product or service.

### Customer Success

Helps a customer make progress and realize value.

These functions may inform each other, but should not be combined carelessly.

In a neutral customer-research interview:

- do not pitch;
- do not challenge the participant;
- do not defend the company;
- do not solve the problem during the interview;
- do not negotiate;
- do not attempt to save a cancellation;
- do not turn the conversation into onboarding or support.

When an urgent support issue appears, acknowledge it, capture it, and route it separately.

---

## 3. Research decision first

Before recruiting or writing questions, define the business decision the research must inform.

Examples:

- Should we pursue this problem?
- Why are trial participants not enrolling?
- Why do customers cancel?
- Which part of onboarding causes friction?
- Which problem should the product team prioritize?
- What language should positioning use?
- What process do customers follow before switching?
- What value do long-term customers continue to receive?

A research project should not begin with “talk to customers” as the only objective.

### Research brief

Define:

- decision;
- known facts;
- assumptions;
- learning questions;
- participant criteria;
- method;
- evidence needed;
- analysis approach;
- decision rule;
- owner;
- time boundary.

---

## 4. Interviews or quantitative data

Use quantitative evidence to understand:

- what is happening;
- how often;
- for whom;
- where in a process;
- how performance changes over time.

Use interviews to understand:

- why behavior occurs;
- how a process works;
- what the person was trying to achieve;
- what tradeoffs mattered;
- how the person interpreted events;
- why a customer switched, continued, or canceled.

Use both when the decision requires magnitude and explanation.

Do not ask an interview participant to estimate a metric that internal data can measure more reliably.

---

## 5. Project-based and ongoing research

### Project-based research

Use for a defined decision, such as:

- evaluating an idea;
- understanding cancellation;
- improving a workflow;
- testing a prototype;
- investigating pricing.

### Ongoing research

Use to maintain current understanding across:

- new customers;
- long-term customers;
- highly engaged customers;
- low-engagement customers;
- canceled customers;
- prospective customers;
- users of alternatives.

Ongoing research should produce a manageable cadence and a shared evidence repository, not an endless interview program without decisions.

---

## 6. Everything is a process

A task that appears simple usually contains multiple steps, decisions, tools, people, delays, workarounds, and emotional states.

Reconstruct:

- what happens first;
- what happens next;
- who participates;
- what information is used;
- where delays occur;
- which workarounds exist;
- where the person becomes uncertain;
- what signals completion;
- what happens afterward.

A valuable opportunity may improve one difficult step rather than replacing the entire process.

---

## 7. Core evidence dimensions

### Process

What sequence does the participant follow?

### Context

Where, when, with whom, and under what constraints does it happen?

### Functional dimension

What is the participant trying to accomplish?

### Emotional dimension

How does the experience make the participant feel?

### Social dimension

How does the experience affect identity, status, trust, responsibility, or how others see the participant?

### Pain

What is difficult, slow, risky, confusing, frustrating, or expensive?

### Frequency

How often does it occur?

### Workaround

What does the person currently do instead?

### Willingness to pay or switch

What evidence shows the problem is important enough to justify money, effort, or change?

### Valuable, usable, viable, feasible

- Valuable: does it matter to the customer?
- Usable: can the person use the solution effectively?
- Viable: can the business support it economically?
- Feasible: can it be delivered reliably?

Customer interviews provide customer evidence. They do not alone prove business viability or technical feasibility.

---

## 8. Participant selection

Recruit people whose experience can answer the research question.

Possible groups:

- prospective customers experiencing the problem;
- people actively using alternatives;
- recent buyers;
- new customers;
- long-term customers;
- highly engaged customers;
- low-engagement customers;
- canceled customers;
- people who considered but did not buy;
- users who submitted feature requests;
- people who encountered a specific workflow.

### Diversity

Seek relevant variation in:

- use case;
- role;
- segment;
- experience level;
- outcome;
- behavior;
- success or failure;
- chosen alternative.

Do not seek demographic diversity as a substitute for decision-relevant variation.

### Sample approach

Begin with a small set, often around five interviews, then analyze and narrow or expand the next cycle.

Do not treat a small qualitative sample as statistically representative.

---

## 9. Recruiting participants

A recruitment message should state:

- who you are;
- why the person was selected;
- what you want to learn;
- expected duration;
- whether recording is requested;
- whether compensation or a gift is offered;
- that the conversation is not a sales call when applicable;
- the requested next step.

### Recruitment principles

- keep the request specific;
- avoid deceptive incentives;
- avoid implying that participation guarantees product changes;
- recruit from the behavior or experience relevant to the study;
- do not overpromise confidentiality or anonymity beyond the actual process.

Possible channels include:

- direct email;
- WhatsApp;
- customer lists;
- short surveys with an interview invitation;
- communities;
- LinkedIn;
- forums;
- support conversations, followed by a separate invitation.

Verify current platform rules before using a channel at scale.

---

## 10. Interview preparation

Before the interview:

- define the research decision;
- review participant context without overfitting assumptions;
- choose a guide rather than a rigid questionnaire;
- prepare a recording and note-taking method;
- obtain permission before recording;
- remove distractions;
- establish the time commitment;
- decide how feature requests or support issues will be captured;
- prepare a thank-you message;
- avoid scheduling too many interviews in one day when quality would decline.

When another team member joins, clarify roles:

- primary interviewer;
- note-taker;
- observer.

Observers should not interrupt the participant unnecessarily.

---

## 11. Interviewer behavior

### Use a gentle tone

The participant should feel that their experience is valid and that the interviewer is not judging performance or intelligence.

### Validate without agreeing

Validation means acknowledging that the participant’s reasoning makes sense from their perspective.

It does not require agreeing with conclusions.

### Allow pauses

Silence may give the participant time to remember or reflect.

Do not fill every pause.

### Mirror and summarize

Repeat important words or summarize the process to show listening and invite correction.

### Do not interrupt

Let the participant complete the story before redirecting.

### Use simple language

Avoid internal terminology, leading jargon, and complex multi-part questions.

### Ask for clarification

Even when the interviewer believes they understand, ask the participant to explain in their own words.

### Do not explain or defend

When the participant criticizes a product, process, or company, do not correct them during neutral research.

### Build on what they say

Follow the participant’s relevant thread rather than mechanically returning to the script.

### Let the participant be the expert

The participant is the authority on their own experience.

---

## 12. Ask about behavior, not hypothetical preference

Prefer:

- Tell me about the last time this happened.
- What did you do next?
- What were you using before?
- When did you first begin considering a change?
- What made you take action?
- How did you decide?
- What did you expect?
- What happened after you started?

Avoid relying on:

- Would you use this?
- Would you pay for this?
- Do you like this idea?
- What features do you want?
- Would this solve your problem?

Hypothetical answers can be useful as directional input but are weaker than specific past behavior.

---

## 13. Avoid leading questions

Leading questions reveal the answer the interviewer wants.

Instead of:

- Was that confusing?

Ask:

- What was going through your mind at that point?
- What did you expect to happen?
- What happened next?

Instead of:

- Would automation save you time?

Ask:

- How do you complete this today?
- Which step takes the most effort?
- What happens when that step fails?

# Part I — Interview guides

## 14. Problem-discovery interview

### Use when

- exploring a problem space;
- validating whether a meaningful process or struggle exists;
- understanding alternatives and workarounds;
- deciding whether to build or offer something.

### Guide

1. Tell me about your role or situation as it relates to this process.
2. Walk me through the last time you did it.
3. What happened first?
4. What happened next?
5. Which parts required the most effort?
6. Where did you get stuck or create a workaround?
7. How often does this happen?
8. What happens when it goes poorly?
9. What have you tried to improve it?
10. What do you use today?
11. What do you like and dislike about that approach?
12. Who else is involved?
13. How important is improving this compared with other priorities?
14. What did I not ask that matters?

Do not introduce the planned solution unless the research design explicitly includes a later concept-testing section.

---

## 15. Switching or new-customer interview

### Use when

- reconstructing why a person recently bought or switched;
- identifying triggers, forces, alternatives, and expectations;
- improving positioning, sales, and onboarding.

### Guide

1. Before you started looking, what were you doing?
2. When did you first think something might need to change?
3. What was happening at that time?
4. What did you do after that first thought?
5. What event made you actively look?
6. Which alternatives did you consider?
7. What did you like about the old way?
8. What worried you about changing?
9. What attracted you to the new option?
10. Who else influenced the decision?
11. Which tradeoffs did you make?
12. What did you expect would improve?
13. What happened during onboarding?
14. What early experience confirmed or weakened the decision?

Capture:

- first thought;
- passive looking;
- triggering event;
- active looking;
- deciding;
- push;
- pull;
- anxiety;
- habit;
- functional, emotional, and social motivations;
- big hire;
- first little hires.

---

## 16. Long-term customer interview

### Use when

- understanding sustained value;
- identifying little hires;
- improving retention and expansion;
- finding advocacy language.

### Guide

1. What were you trying to accomplish when you first started?
2. How has your use changed over time?
3. Which parts do you rely on most?
4. Tell me about the last time the product or service was especially useful.
5. What would be difficult if it disappeared tomorrow?
6. Which workarounds or alternatives would you consider?
7. Where does the experience still require effort?
8. Who else receives value?
9. What keeps you continuing?
10. What nearly caused you to stop?
11. What result do you recognize today that you did not have before?
12. How would you describe the value to someone similar to you?

Do not ask for a testimonial during the research interview. Make advocacy a separate request after the interview when appropriate.

---

## 17. Cancellation interview

### Use when

- understanding churn;
- distinguishing preventable, structural, and bad-fit cancellation;
- identifying early risk signals;
- improving acquisition, onboarding, product, service, or pricing.

### Separation rule

A cancellation interview is research, not a save conversation.

If retention recovery is desired, schedule it separately and make the change in purpose explicit.

### Guide

1. When did you first begin thinking about stopping?
2. What was happening at that time?
3. What had you expected when you started?
4. Which expectations were met?
5. Which expectations were not met?
6. Tell me about the events leading to the decision.
7. What alternatives did you consider?
8. Was there a particular triggering event?
9. Which part of the experience mattered most?
10. Did cost play a role? How?
11. Did usage, schedule, fit, implementation, support, or results play a role?
12. What are you doing instead?
13. What might have changed the outcome earlier?
14. Is there anything else we should understand?

Capture:

- stated reason;
- deeper cause;
- timeline;
- early signal;
- original expectation;
- value realized;
- value recognized;
- alternative chosen;
- preventability;
- system implication.

---

## 18. Interactive interview

### Use when

- observing a prototype, product, document, workflow, or interface;
- understanding expectations and behavior;
- identifying usability friction.

### Behavior

Ask the participant to narrate what they expect and what they are doing.

Use prompts such as:

- What do you think this is?
- What would you do next?
- What are you looking for?
- What did you expect to happen?
- What is going through your mind?

Avoid labeling something as confusing before the participant does.

Do not train the participant during the observation unless the test specifically requires assisted use.

---

## 19. Prioritization interview

### Use when

- comparing problems, tasks, or possible areas of investment;
- understanding relative importance;
- avoiding roadmap decisions based only on request volume.

Possible approach:

- present problem or task cards;
- ask the participant to group and rank them;
- explore why items are important;
- ask about frequency and consequences;
- identify which items the participant already pays or works around.

Do not use prioritization research as a public promise that the top item will be built.

---

## 20. Willingness-to-pay research

Direct hypothetical price questions are weak when unsupported by context.

Prefer evidence such as:

- what the participant pays today;
- cost of the workaround;
- budget ownership;
- approval thresholds;
- previous purchases;
- cost of the problem;
- tradeoffs made;
- reaction to realistic packages.

Possible questions:

- What are you paying for the current approach?
- Who controls that budget?
- What happened the last time you bought something similar?
- What would make the investment clearly worthwhile?
- At what point would it become difficult to justify?
- Which part of the offer changes your willingness to pay?

Separate customer evidence from final pricing decisions, which also require costs, positioning, and economics.

---

## 21. Feature-request interview

A requested feature is a proposed solution, not necessarily the underlying problem.

Explore:

1. Tell me about the last time you needed this.
2. What were you trying to accomplish?
3. What happened?
4. What did you do instead?
5. How often does this occur?
6. Who else is affected?
7. What is the consequence?
8. Why does the requested feature appear to solve it?
9. What would success look like?

Record:

- problem;
- use case;
- frequency;
- impact;
- workaround;
- segment;
- requested solution;
- evidence.

Do not commit to the feature during the interview.

# Part II — Analysis

## 22. Evidence capture

Capture:

- participant characteristics relevant to the decision;
- direct notes;
- exact customer language when useful;
- process steps;
- events;
- behaviors;
- workarounds;
- emotional and social context;
- contradictions;
- hypotheses generated;
- support issues requiring separate routing.

Obtain permission before recording.

Avoid collecting unnecessary sensitive information.

---

## 23. Interview debrief

Immediately after each interview:

- summarize the process;
- record the most important evidence;
- identify surprising information;
- note interviewer mistakes;
- distinguish observation from interpretation;
- update hypotheses;
- identify questions for the next interview.

Do not wait until all interviews are complete before noticing that the guide is failing.

---

## 24. Journey mapping

Map:

- stages;
- actions;
- people;
- tools;
- information;
- emotions;
- problems;
- workarounds;
- decision points;
- outcomes.

For switching research, include:

- first thought;
- passive looking;
- triggering event;
- active looking;
- deciding;
- onboarding;
- ongoing use.

---

## 25. Pain and frequency matrix

Evaluate problems by:

- intensity or consequence;
- frequency;
- segment;
- workaround;
- willingness to pay;
- strategic fit.

A frequent low-intensity problem and an infrequent severe problem may require different solutions.

Do not reduce qualitative evidence to a score without preserving context.

---

## 26. Pattern analysis

Across interviews, identify:

- repeated process steps;
- common triggers;
- shared language;
- recurring problems;
- meaningful differences by segment or use case;
- common alternatives;
- repeated anxieties;
- divergent behavior;
- negative cases that challenge the emerging conclusion.

Do not report a pattern simply because two participants used similar words without similar context.

---

## 27. Analysis output

A research synthesis should include:

1. Decision the research was designed to inform
2. Participants and limitations
3. Confirmed evidence
4. Repeated patterns
5. Relevant differences
6. Customer process or journey
7. Functional, emotional, and social dimensions
8. Problems, frequency, and consequences
9. Workarounds and alternatives
10. Customer language
11. Hypotheses supported
12. Hypotheses weakened
13. Unknowns
14. Immediate actions
15. Further research
16. Decision permitted by the evidence

Do not claim statistical representativeness from qualitative interviews.

---

## 28. Bias controls

Watch for:

- confirmation bias;
- leading questions;
- overvaluing enthusiastic participants;
- recruiting only happy customers;
- treating stated preference as behavior;
- defending the product;
- explaining features;
- interrupting;
- ignoring negative cases;
- asking several questions at once;
- interpreting silence as agreement;
- confusing support demand with market demand.

A research guide should include interviewer reminders when the risk is high.

---

## 29. Research loops

Use iterative cycles:

1. define decision;
2. interview;
3. debrief;
4. analyze;
5. narrow or revise questions;
6. interview the next set;
7. decide or continue.

Stop or change direction when:

- the decision can be made with acceptable confidence;
- new interviews no longer add decision-relevant variation;
- the participant set is wrong;
- the research question changes;
- quantitative evidence is now required;
- the solution is technically or economically infeasible.

---

## 30. Company-specific adaptation

The user's own companies differ in model, buyer and stage, so this playbook stays general. When the company's folder in the context folder holds `method-adaptation.md`, read the part about this subject before recommending: it records how the method applies to that company (who to interview, what constrains growth, which practices are too heavy for it). When there is no such file and the adaptation matters, work it out from the company's context file and offer to record it there.

---

## 31. When to consult the original source

Consult *Deploy Empathy* when:

- creating a detailed interview script;
- designing recruitment for a difficult participant group;
- handling short answers, nervous participants, anger, feature fixation, or off-topic discussion;
- designing cancellation, pricing, prioritization, or interactive interviews;
- improving interviewer skill;
- analyzing a full transcript;
- distinguishing support, sales, and research in an edge case.

Consult *Demand-Side Sales 101* when:

- reconstructing the detailed switching timeline;
- identifying Forces of Progress;
- analyzing first thought, passive looking, active looking, deciding, onboarding, or ongoing use.

Preserve neutral research behavior even when Demand-Side concepts are used.

---

## 32. Research quality check

Before finalizing a research plan or synthesis, verify that:

- a specific business decision is defined;
- participants match the decision;
- neutral research is separated from sales and support;
- questions concern real behavior and process;
- leading language is minimized;
- recording permission and privacy are handled;
- participant context and limitations are stated;
- evidence is separated from interpretation;
- patterns preserve relevant differences;
- qualitative findings are not presented as statistical proof;
- the output leads to a decision, experiment, or next research question.
