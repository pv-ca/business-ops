# Revenue Operations and Scale Playbook

## 1. Purpose

This file is the operating reference for:

- scale readiness;
- recurring-revenue architecture;
- lifecycle governance;
- segment economics;
- roles and specialization;
- capacity planning;
- Revenue Operations;
- data quality;
- pipeline governance;
- forecasting;
- CAC and retention cost;
- profitability;
- dashboards;
- technology selection;
- ABM operating systems;
- organizational learning.

The principal source foundations are:

- *Blueprints for a SaaS Sales Organization*;
- *Account-Based Marketing*;
- *The SaaS Sales Method Fundamentals*;
- supporting commercial and Customer Success principles.

This playbook should not be used to impose a scaled organization on an unvalidated business model.

---

# Part I — Scale readiness

## 2. Scale is not validation

Scale increases the output of an existing system. It does not prove that the system is correct.

Before recommending scaled acquisition, specialization, automation, ABM expansion, or additional management layers, determine whether the business has enough evidence of:

- a defined customer problem;
- a sufficiently clear offer;
- real demand;
- a repeatable use case;
- a segment or target-market hypothesis;
- a reasonably repeatable commercial motion;
- delivery consistency;
- customer value;
- retention or continuation;
- basic unit economics;
- operational capacity.

When these conditions are absent, prioritize:

- customer research;
- founder-led sales;
- controlled pilots;
- offer refinement;
- delivery learning;
- simple instrumentation;
- validation.

---

## 3. Scale-readiness assessment

Score each area as:

- `unknown`;
- `weak_evidence`;
- `emerging`;
- `repeatable`;
- `scalable`.

### Market and customer

- recurring problem pattern;
- identifiable segment;
- clear buyer or customer;
- meaningful urgency;
- known alternatives;
- evidence of willingness to change.

### Offer

- defined outcome;
- consistent scope;
- credible differentiation;
- pricing logic;
- manageable implementation;
- clear exclusions.

### Commercial motion

- repeatable acquisition source;
- qualification criteria;
- discovery method;
- conversion evidence;
- understandable sales cycle;
- documented loss reasons.

### Delivery

- reliable onboarding;
- capacity visibility;
- activation hypothesis;
- service quality;
- support model;
- known dependencies.

### Retention

- evidence of recurring use or continuation;
- known reasons for churn;
- customer value recognized;
- manageable cost to serve.

### Economics

- revenue model;
- gross-margin understanding;
- acquisition cost estimate;
- implementation and retention cost;
- time to profitability;
- cash-flow requirements.

### Data and management

- lifecycle definitions;
- system of record;
- basic data quality;
- ownership;
- decision cadence.

Do not aggregate the assessment into a false-precision score when qualitative differences matter.

---

## 4. Scale decision

### Discovery or validation stage

Use:

- direct founder involvement;
- research;
- small experiments;
- manual processes that accelerate learning;
- minimal tools;
- narrow offers.

### Emerging repeatability

Use:

- documented stages;
- simple playbooks;
- a small number of metrics;
- consistent handoffs;
- basic capacity planning;
- selective automation.

### Scale stage

Use:

- specialized roles when economically justified;
- RevOps governance;
- formal lifecycle definitions;
- forecasting;
- instrumentation;
- segment-specific motions;
- scalable enablement;
- deliberate technology.

# Part II — Customer journey and Moments That Matter

## 5. Customer-centric journey

A recurring-revenue business should organize around the customer’s journey, not only the seller’s funnel.

A useful high-level model includes:

1. Awareness
2. Education
3. Selection
4. Commit
5. Onboarding
6. Use and Expansion

The exact stages should be adapted to the business model.

---

## 6. Moment That Matters definition

A Moment That Matters is a stage or interaction that materially influences customer progress, commercial conversion, value realization, or retention.

For each moment define:

- customer objective;
- customer questions;
- company action;
- owner;
- required information;
- entry criteria;
- exit criteria;
- expected next step;
- metric;
- risk;
- handoff.

Do not create stages that represent only internal activity.

---

## 7. Awareness

Customer objective:

- recognize a relevant problem or opportunity.

Company responsibilities may include:

- research-backed positioning;
- useful education;
- relevant channels;
- credible customer language.

Possible measures:

- target audience reached;
- relevant engagement;
- first-thought creation;
- qualified inbound behavior.

Avoid optimizing reach without relevance.

---

## 8. Education

Customer objective:

- understand the problem, alternatives, tradeoffs, and possible approaches.

Company responsibilities may include:

- insight;
- evidence;
- problem education;
- buyer guidance;
- decision criteria.

This stage may use Demand-Side or Challenger methods depending on complexity and evidence.

Do not confuse education with a feature dump.

---

## 9. Selection

Customer objective:

- evaluate fit, value, risk, and alternatives.

Company responsibilities may include:

- qualification;
- discovery;
- demonstration or trial;
- proof;
- stakeholder alignment;
- implementation clarity;
- commercial proposal.

Possible measures:

- stage conversion;
- time in stage;
- decision criteria coverage;
- stakeholder coverage;
- loss reasons.

---

## 10. Commit

Customer objective:

- make and formalize a decision.

Company responsibilities may include:

- commercial terms;
- mutual expectations;
- ownership;
- scope;
- risk clarity;
- handoff readiness.

A contract or payment is not proof of future value.

---

## 11. Onboarding

Customer objective:

- begin using the solution and reach first meaningful value.

Company responsibilities may include:

- implementation;
- customer education;
- activation;
- expectation management;
- risk detection.

Possible measures:

- implementation duration;
- activation;
- time to value;
- milestone completion;
- risk incidence.

---

## 12. Use and Expansion

Customer objective:

- continue making progress and decide whether to continue, deepen, or replace the relationship.

Company responsibilities may include:

- adoption;
- value recognition;
- support;
- risk management;
- renewal;
- appropriate expansion;
- advocacy.

Possible measures:

- meaningful use;
- retention;
- renewal;
- expansion;
- contraction;
- churn;
- referrals.

---

# Part III — Segment economics and service tiers

## 13. Segment before structuring the organization

The sales and service model should fit the economics and complexity of the segment.

Evaluate:

- number of potential customers;
- revenue per customer;
- gross margin;
- sales cycle;
- stakeholder count;
- implementation effort;
- support demand;
- retention potential;
- expansion potential;
- acquisition channel;
- required expertise.

Do not use expensive, high-touch resources for low-value, high-volume segments without an explicit economic reason.

Do not force low-touch service on complex high-risk customers.

---

## 14. Tier design

A tier should represent a real difference in:

- customer need;
- value;
- complexity;
- commercial motion;
- implementation;
- service level;
- economics.

Possible motions include:

- self-service or assisted online;
- inside sales;
- field or enterprise sales;
- pooled Customer Success;
- dedicated Customer Success;
- partner-led.

Do not copy historical tier thresholds from a book. Derive tiers from current economics and customer behavior.

---

## 15. Tier-level profitability

Calculate profitability separately by relevant tier, segment, or vertical when costs and behavior differ materially.

Do not blend:

- high-velocity low-value accounts;
- complex enterprise accounts;
- different implementation burdens;
- different retention profiles;

into one average that hides an unprofitable motion.

---

# Part IV — Unit economics

## 16. Revenue, cash, and profit

Distinguish:

- booked revenue;
- recurring revenue;
- cash collected;
- gross profit;
- contribution margin;
- operating profit.

A signed contract can still be economically harmful when acquisition, implementation, service, and retention costs exceed expected contribution.

---

## 17. Customer Acquisition Cost

Define CAC explicitly.

A practical calculation may include:

- sales salaries and commissions;
- marketing salaries;
- media and programs;
- content;
- events;
- tools;
- relevant overhead;
- time period aligned with the sales cycle.

### Basic structure

Acquisition costs attributable to a cohort divided by customers acquired in that cohort.

Align costs and acquired customers across the same causal period.

Do not use one month of cost against deals generated by a much longer cycle without adjustment.

---

## 18. Customer retention cost

Retention cost may include:

- Customer Success people;
- account management;
- support portions associated with retention;
- professional services;
- training;
- customer education;
- community;
- engagement tools;
- customer marketing.

Define what is included and why.

Do not adopt an old rule-of-thumb acquisition-to-retention ratio as current fact without verification.

---

## 19. Time to profitability

Estimate how long it takes the contribution from a customer to recover:

- acquisition cost;
- implementation cost;
- ongoing service and retention cost.

Use current margin and cash timing.

Do not rely only on lifetime value when retention assumptions are unstable.

### Decision use

Time to profitability can inform:

- pricing;
- contract terms;
- segment choice;
- channel;
- service model;
- capacity;
- cash requirements.

---

## 20. Expansion economics

Expansion may improve profitability through:

- more users;
- more usage;
- higher plan;
- additional workflow;
- cross-sell;
- price adjustment.

Separate:

- upsell to the same stakeholder or use case;
- cross-sell to another stakeholder or use case;
- price increase;
- organic usage growth.

Do not assume expansion will compensate for weak retention.

---

# Part V — Lifecycle and process governance

## 21. Lifecycle stages

Stages should represent verifiable customer progress.

For each stage define:

- name;
- purpose;
- entry criteria;
- exit criteria;
- evidence;
- owner;
- required fields;
- next action;
- maximum or expected age;
- exception process.

Do not define a stage only by seller activity such as “proposal sent.”

---

## 22. Handoffs

A handoff should transfer the context needed for the next function to succeed.

Relevant handoffs may include:

- Marketing to Sales;
- SDR to AE;
- Sales to implementation;
- implementation to Customer Success;
- Support to Product;
- Customer Success to renewal or expansion;
- customer feedback to Product and Marketing.

For each handoff define:

- trigger;
- information;
- acceptance criteria;
- owner;
- timing;
- rejection or correction process.

Do not transfer work without transferring context.

---

## 23. Service-level expectations

Use service-level expectations when coordination problems justify them.

Examples:

- response time;
- lead acceptance;
- handoff completeness;
- implementation start;
- risk escalation;
- follow-up.

Avoid artificial service levels that reward speed while reducing quality.

---

# Part VI — Roles and organizational design

## 24. Role design principle

Create a role when a coherent body of work has sufficient volume, economic value, and specialization need.

Before adding a role, define:

- work to be owned;
- required outcome;
- current owner;
- volume;
- skill requirement;
- capacity constraint;
- expected economic effect;
- handoffs;
- performance measures;
- cost.

Do not hire because a common SaaS org chart contains the role.

---

## 25. Possible commercial and customer roles

Depending on stage and economics:

- founder-led sales;
- marketing or demand generation;
- SDR or BDR;
- account executive;
- sales engineer;
- implementation specialist;
- Customer Success manager;
- account manager;
- support specialist;
- Revenue Operations;
- customer marketing;
- partner manager.

One person may own several functions early. Specialize when the benefits exceed coordination cost.

---

## 26. Founder-led sales

Founder-led sales is often appropriate during discovery and validation because founders can:

- learn directly;
- change the offer quickly;
- identify patterns;
- build early credibility;
- make product decisions.

Before transferring sales to a team, document:

- segment;
- problem;
- qualification;
- discovery;
- offer;
- proof;
- decision process;
- objections;
- successful patterns;
- disqualification.

Do not attempt to hire around an unknown motion.

---

## 27. Specialization risks

Premature specialization can create:

- excessive handoffs;
- loss of customer context;
- unclear ownership;
- high fixed cost;
- low role utilization;
- process before learning.

Late specialization can create:

- overloaded people;
- inconsistent execution;
- slow response;
- weak expertise;
- poor forecasting;
- founder bottlenecks.

Use workload and economics to determine timing.

---

## 28. Pod or team-unit design

A pod may combine related roles around a segment or revenue goal.

Use only when:

- the motion is repeatable;
- roles have sufficient workload;
- ownership is clear;
- metrics are coherent;
- the unit can be replicated.

Do not copy historical productivity or staffing numbers from examples as targets.

---

# Part VII — Revenue Operations

## 29. RevOps purpose

Revenue Operations creates a coherent operating system across:

- Marketing;
- Sales;
- implementation;
- Customer Success;
- renewal;
- expansion;
- Finance.

It may govern:

- lifecycle definitions;
- process;
- ownership;
- data;
- systems;
- reporting;
- forecasting;
- capacity;
- planning;
- enablement;
- governance.

RevOps should improve decisions and execution, not become an administrative layer disconnected from customers.

---

## 30. System of record

Define where authoritative information lives.

For each key object or field, specify:

- definition;
- source;
- owner;
- update rule;
- quality check;
- downstream use.

Avoid collecting data that no decision or process uses.

---

## 31. Data quality

Data should be sufficiently:

- accurate;
- complete;
- timely;
- consistent;
- traceable;
- actionable.

For a data-quality problem, identify:

- field or object;
- business decision affected;
- root cause;
- owner;
- correction;
- prevention.

Do not solve every data problem with mandatory fields. Excessive fields can reduce accuracy.

---

## 32. Reporting design

Before creating a report or dashboard, define:

- audience;
- decision;
- metric;
- formula;
- source;
- owner;
- cadence;
- threshold;
- action.

A dashboard is not useful merely because the data is available.

---

## 33. Funnel and lifecycle metrics

Possible metrics include:

- relevant reach;
- leads;
- qualified leads;
- meetings or trials scheduled;
- attendance;
- opportunities;
- proposals;
- wins;
- conversion by stage;
- cycle time;
- stage age;
- acquisition cost;
- implementation duration;
- activation;
- time to value;
- retention;
- expansion;
- churn;
- capacity.

Choose the smallest set that identifies the constraint and supports action.

---

## 34. Forecasting

A forecast should combine:

- verified stage evidence;
- customer commitments;
- stakeholders;
- decision process;
- timing;
- risks;
- historical conversion;
- seller judgment.

Separate:

- total pipeline;
- upside or best case;
- commit;
- closed.

Do not forecast from stage label or seller confidence alone.

---

## 35. Capacity planning

Model capacity using:

- demand volume;
- work per unit;
- available people or hours;
- skill constraints;
- peak periods;
- utilization;
- quality requirements;
- variability;
- rework;
- administrative overhead.

In a service business the people who deliver and their schedules usually set the limit; in a software business, implementation, support and product capacity do.

Do not increase acquisition without checking delivery capacity.

---

# Part VIII — ABM operating system

## 36. ABM purpose

Account-Based Marketing is a B2B strategy that aligns commercial and marketing effort around selected accounts with meaningful economic value.

The two central measurement anchors are:

- accounts;
- revenue.

ABM should be a strategy and operating model, not a technology purchase.

---

## 37. ABM maturity

Assess maturity across:

- target-account selection;
- Sales and Marketing alignment;
- programs and engagement;
- measurement;
- technology;
- organizational adoption.

Begin with the current operating reality rather than an idealized model.

---

## 38. ABM pilot team

A pilot may require:

- executive sponsor;
- ABM owner;
- sales champion;
- marketing champion;
- account owners;
- operations or analytics support;
- relevant content or field support.

In a small company, one person may hold several roles. Responsibilities must still be explicit.

---

## 39. Target-account list governance

The list is central to the program.

Define:

- owner;
- account-selection criteria;
- data sources;
- Sales and Marketing input;
- provisional agreement;
- tiers or segments;
- review cadence;
- additions and removals;
- relationship and stakeholder data;
- account objective.

A target-account list should not change constantly according to individual seller preference without governance.

---

## 40. ABM six-area model

### Identify

Select and govern the right accounts.

### Attract

Reach relevant people at selected accounts with coordinated awareness.

### Engage

Deliver relevant messages, content, experiences, and interactions across stakeholders.

### Convert

Use meaningful signals to create qualified commercial engagement.

### Close

Support consensus, decision progression, and commercial execution.

### Measure

Evaluate account engagement, pipeline, revenue, conversion, and learning.

---

## 41. ABM engagement

Account engagement should reflect meaningful behavior, not only impressions.

Possible signals:

- relevant website activity;
- content engagement;
- event participation;
- stakeholder interaction;
- meeting activity;
- opportunity progression;
- response to account-specific communication.

Different stakeholders in the same account may be at different stages.

Use consistent messages across channels while tailoring to role and context.

---

## 42. ABM conversion and closing

Define which signals justify sales action.

Avoid routing every anonymous or low-intent interaction as an urgent sales lead.

For active accounts:

- map stakeholders;
- identify champion and decision-maker;
- understand consensus requirements;
- use relevant insight;
- coordinate next actions;
- track commercial progress.

---

## 43. ABM measurement

Possible measures:

- target accounts engaged;
- stakeholder coverage;
- target accounts visiting relevant assets;
- meaningful interactions;
- meetings;
- opportunities;
- percentage of pipeline from target accounts;
- pipeline generation;
- stage progression;
- conversion;
- sales cycle;
- average deal size;
- closed revenue;
- expansion;
- pilot ROI or learning.

Do not evaluate ABM only by leads, impressions, or clicks.

### Baselines

Establish a baseline before the pilot when possible.

Compare:

- target-account performance;
- non-target or historical performance;
- cost and effort;
- quality of opportunities;
- revenue outcomes.

Avoid causal claims when the pilot design cannot support them.

---

## 44. ABM scaling

Scale only after the pilot produces useful evidence.

If results are weak, investigate:

- account selection;
- segment fit;
- problem hypothesis;
- message;
- stakeholder coverage;
- channel execution;
- sales follow-up;
- measurement;
- duration;
- operational capacity.

Possible scale paths:

- expand accounts within the same segment;
- add a new segment;
- increase personalization;
- add channels;
- add technology;
- add people.

Choose the path that addresses a proven constraint.

---

# Part IX — Technology and automation

## 45. Strategy before technology

Do not buy technology to create a strategy.

Before selecting a tool, define:

- problem;
- process;
- owner;
- use case;
- data;
- integration;
- required outcome;
- adoption plan;
- cost;
- expected return;
- replacement or exit risk.

Use manual execution during a pilot when it accelerates learning.

---

## 46. Technology gap assessment

After a manual or lightly tooled process, identify where technology could improve:

- account selection;
- data quality;
- routing;
- engagement;
- orchestration;
- reporting;
- attribution;
- forecasting;
- enablement;
- customer education.

Prioritize gaps by business impact and repeatability.

Do not add a tool for an infrequent problem that lacks an owner.

---

## 47. Automation policy

Automate processes that are:

- understood;
- repeatable;
- sufficiently stable;
- low-risk or appropriately controlled;
- measurable.

Preserve human judgment where:

- context is ambiguous;
- trust matters;
- communication is sensitive;
- decisions are high-value;
- customer needs vary materially;
- errors are consequential.

---

# Part X — Learning and enablement

## 48. Self-learning organization

A scalable revenue system should learn from current execution.

Create loops that capture:

- customer language;
- objections;
- loss reasons;
- successful discovery;
- implementation risks;
- churn causes;
- account patterns;
- experiment results;
- playbook changes.

Learning should update:

- training;
- messaging;
- qualification;
- process;
- product;
- onboarding;
- documentation.

Do not depend only on annual training or individual “rainmakers.”

---

## 49. Enablement

Enablement should support observable performance.

Possible components:

- role expectations;
- competencies;
- playbooks;
- examples;
- practice;
- coaching;
- call or meeting review;
- certification when useful;
- reinforcement;
- feedback.

Do not measure enablement only by attendance or content completion.

---

## 50. Experimentation

When uncertainty remains and the action is reversible, define:

- hypothesis;
- target segment;
- intervention;
- owner;
- metric;
- threshold;
- time or sample boundary;
- decision rule;
- next action for each result.

Do not call activity an experiment without a decision rule.

---

# Part XI — Company-specific adaptation

## 51. Reading the company's own adaptation

The user's own companies differ in model, buyer and stage, so this playbook stays general. When the company's folder in the context folder holds `adaptacao-dos-metodos.md`, read the part about this subject before recommending: it records how the method applies to that company (who to interview, what constrains growth, which practices are too heavy for it). When there is no such file and the adaptation matters, work it out from the company's context file and offer to record it there.

---

## 53. Scale-plan output

When asked for a scale plan, provide:

1. Current stage and evidence
2. Constraint
3. Scale-readiness gaps
4. Segment economics
5. Customer journey and Moments That Matter
6. Required process changes
7. Role or capacity changes
8. Data and metrics
9. Technology only where justified
10. Risks
11. Pilot or phase structure
12. Decision gates
13. First action

---

## 54. When to consult original sources

Consult *Blueprints for a SaaS Sales Organization* when:

- designing a complete SaaS revenue organization;
- analyzing segment tiers and economics;
- connecting acquisition, onboarding, retention, and expansion;
- designing roles, pods, or a Sales Operations Center;
- examining CAC, retention cost, or time to profitability in source detail.

Consult *Account-Based Marketing* when:

- designing the target-account list and governance;
- building an ABM pilot;
- designing measurement dashboards;
- scaling a proven ABM program;
- evaluating ABM technology.

Consult *The SaaS Sales Method Fundamentals* when:

- designing detailed interactions and handoffs between meetings;
- creating communication enablement.

Treat historical benchmarks, named tools, generational claims, and old channel tactics as time-bound.

---

## 55. Quality check

Before finalizing RevOps or scale advice, verify that:

- scale readiness was assessed;
- validation is not being mistaken for scale;
- segment economics determine the motion;
- lifecycle stages represent customer progress;
- handoffs transfer context;
- roles have sufficient workload and economic justification;
- metrics have definitions, owners, and actions;
- CAC and retention costs use consistent periods and definitions;
- profitability is not hidden by blended tiers;
- ABM begins with account selection and a pilot;
- technology follows strategy and process;
- current benchmarks are verified when needed;
- delivery capacity can absorb growth;
- facts, hypotheses, and unknowns remain separate.
